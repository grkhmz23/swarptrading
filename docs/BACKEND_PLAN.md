# Build a swap wallet, not an exchange

A small TypeScript team can realistically rebuild the lost backend as a multi-chain wallet whose "exchange" function is swaps routed through third-party aggregators; a licensed order-book exchange that holds customer funds is not a realistic first product for this team. The recommended shape is embedded wallets from a wallet-as-a-service vendor (Privy first, Turnkey as fallback) rooted in each user's own login, with Solana live first and the EVM family, Bitcoin and Tron added in that order behind one chain-neutral API. The existing front end is custodial by design, because the server signs after checking a PIN, and that model needs a licence in nearly every market the notes cover: MiCA authorisation in the EU, where every transitional period ended on 1 July 2026, and money-transmitter status in the US. The researchers estimate 4-6 months for a Solana-first MVP with 3-4 engineers and roughly 7-12 months to reach four chain families; these are low-to-medium-confidence estimates, not benchmarks from comparable teams. Costed running lines come to about $700-2,150 a month at roughly 1,000 monthly users and about $20,000-24,000 at roughly 50,000, on this report's arithmetic from list prices and stated assumptions. Almost every vendor figure below comes from web-search summaries because vendor sites could not be opened, several figures conflict, and each one must be re-checked on the live page before a contract or build decision. The two decisions that gate everything else are the custody model and the jurisdiction, and the regulatory material here is background for a conversation with counsel, not legal advice.

## Most figures here are search summaries, and several conflict

Three grades of evidence sit behind this report, and they should not be read as equal. The first grade is direct reading: the researchers read the front-end repository itself (the dashboard client, the admin console, the preview API, the requirements file and the audit) and fetched npm registry data live on 22 November 2026, so the API inventory and the library versions are primary. The second grade, which covers nearly all vendor pricing, chain coverage, fee mechanics and regulation, is **web-search result summaries of the linked pages, not the pages themselves**: every direct fetch of a vendor documentation site failed because the hosts were outside the session's allowed network domains, and the shared search budget ran out before the last round of queries in every topic. The third grade is the researchers' own inference, estimate or recollection.

This report keeps those grades visible. A claim followed by a link is a sourced statement as summarised by search on 22 November 2026. A claim marked "researcher estimate" or "researcher inference" comes from the notes' reasoning, and a claim marked "this report's judgement" or "this report's arithmetic" is synthesis done here; none of those is a sourced fact. Items the researchers flagged as unverified recollection (for example CAIP-19 reference values, Meteora curve parameters, US state licensing cost, the cost of a legal opinion, MoonPay's consumer card fee) are either left out or labelled where they appear.

The practical consequence is that **no price, rate limit, chain list or fee share in this report should go into a contract, a budget commitment or a design decision until someone has read the live vendor page**. The last section lists the specific numbers where sources conflict. Topics the notes did not reach at all include: fully non-custodial connect-wallet kits, RPC coverage for Bitcoin and Tron, typical settlement and refund times for most cross-chain providers, hosting prices on AWS and Google Cloud, bug-bounty and insurance costs, email and push providers, and the size of the front-end rework. Whether the launchpad and staking on-chain programs already exist, and whether there are existing users whose wallets or keys must be migrated, cannot be told from the repository.

## The realistic product is an aggregator-routed wallet on embedded keys

"Multi-chain exchange" can mean four different products, and they differ far more in licensing and risk than in features. The table sets them out; the verdict column is this report's judgement from the evidence that follows.

| Option | What it is | Fit with the existing front end | Licensing consequence (background, not advice) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Custodial order-book exchange | Company holds funds, runs matching and liquidity | Not what the front end is; nothing in the notes covers matching engines or liquidity | Custody plus trading-platform authorisation; the researchers place a trading platform in MiCA's top EUR 150k capital class from recollection of the annex | Not realistic as a first product |
| Custodial wallet with aggregator swaps | Today's design extended to more chains: server signs after a PIN check | Closest fit; keeps "server signs automatically" flows | Licensed almost everywhere: MiCA CASP in the EU, money transmitter in the US, store licensing rules | Possible only with a funded licence programme of 9-18 months |
| Embedded wallets (wallet-as-a-service) with aggregator swaps | Vendor enclave holds keys; each wallet is rooted in the user's own authentication; backend prepares and tracks | Keeps phone or Google sign-in and in-app swaps; changes who signs | Depends on configuration; favourable US staff positions, unresolved in EU and UK | **Recommended** |
| Pure front end, user brings a wallet | No keys at all | Breaks phone-and-PIN onboarding | Lowest custody risk; still subject to sanctions and promotions rules | Not researched; not recommended as the main path |

The existing product is custodial under every provider's own definition, whichever vendor holds the keys, because the developer's backend can cause a signature on its own; a server-checked 6-digit PIN is an app-layer gate, not a user-held cryptographic factor (researcher inference from the provider documentation summarised in the notes). That matters because custody is the trigger for licensing. MiCA defines custody as safekeeping or controlling crypto-assets "or the means of access" on behalf of clients ([Ganado Advocates](https://ganado.com/custody-under-mica-whos-really-in-control/)), and ESMA stated that transitional periods expire across the EU on 1 July 2026, with unauthorised providers expected to have wound down ([ESMA statement, April 2026](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-04/ESMA75-113276571-1679_Statement_on_the_end_of_transitional_periods_under_MiCA.pdf)). A vendor guide puts MiCA capital classes at EUR 50k, 125k and 150k and a realistic path to an operating licence at **9-18 months** ([Crassula](https://crassula.io/guides/licenses/mica-casp/)). In the US, FinCEN's 2019 guidance treats hosted wallet providers as money transmitters ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2019/06/fincen-consolidates-guidance)). Google Play requires licences for custodial wallets and exchanges in 15 jurisdictions and says non-custodial wallets are out of scope ([Google Play Help Center](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16329703?hl=en)).

Embedded wallets are recommended because two vendors, Privy and Turnkey, document both models on the same wallet: user-authenticated signing, plus an optional server signer constrained by policy ([Privy session signers](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/using-wallets/session-signers/configure-session-signers); [Turnkey delegated access](https://docs.turnkey.com/features/policies/delegated-access/overview)). That gives a product that keeps the consumer experience, removes raw keys from the team's servers, and leaves a path in either direction once counsel has spoken. It does not settle the legal question. Providers tie "non-custodial" to signer configuration rather than to the product, and a US law-firm analysis warns that the mere ability to block or authorise transactions can be enough for money-transmitter treatment in some states ([S. Horowitz](https://s-horowitz.com/multi-party-computation-mpc-wallets-and-the-evolving-regulatory-landscape-in-the-united-states/)). No regulator guidance addressing MPC shares, enclave signing or delegated signers by name was found.

The "exchange" part should be routing, not matching. The large consumer wallets already work this way: Phantom's cross-chain swaps use LI.FI, Relay, deBridge and Garden and pick the lowest-priced route by default ([Phantom help](https://help.phantom.com/articles/understanding-cross-chain-swaps-in-phantom-51384804802195)), and Jupiter's own bridge product aggregates Mayan, Relay, NEAR Intents and Circle CCTP rather than running a bridge ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/news/jupiter-bridge-launches-in-beta-comparing-five-bridge-providers-for-transfers-in-and-out-of-solana)).

### Chain order: Solana, then EVM, then Bitcoin, then Tron

Solana comes first because the front end, the launchpad, staking and the token are all Solana-shaped today. The EVM family comes second because one address and one adapter cover many chains, and both shortlisted wallet vendors give EVM and Solana their top support tier ([Turnkey networks overview](https://docs.turnkey.com/networks/overview); [Privy chains](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/overview/chains)). Bitcoin comes third: Relay states it supports cross-chain swaps across EVM, Solana and Bitcoin combinations ([Relay app fees](https://docs.relay.link/features/app-fees)), but no source found any fee-payer abstraction for Bitcoin, so the app must handle fees itself (researcher inference). Tron comes last because support is thinnest: deBridge is the only shortlisted cross-chain provider with a confirmed, documented Tron integration ([deBridge supported chains](https://docs.debridge.com/home/architecture/supported-chains)), most wallet vendors offer Tron only at signing-curve depth, and Privy's Tron gas sponsorship is the one documented exception ([Privy gas sponsorship](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/gas-and-asset-management/gas/overview)). This order is this report's judgement based on integration cost; the notes contain no data on which chains the owner's users actually want, and which EVM networks to enable first is a market question for the owner.

### Signing design decides custody status, not the vendor

The same wallet product can be configured as user-owned, server-in-the-loop or fully service-controlled, so custody is a decision the company makes when it configures the vendor ([Privy policy and controls](https://docs.privy.io/security/wallet-infrastructure/policy-and-controls)). Privy says a signature is produced only when the user provides valid authentication, and a server signer is an added key quorum that the wallet must be configured with ([Privy security architecture](https://docs.privy.io/guide/security/architecture)). Turnkey describes backend-configured delegated access as "generally used only in enterprise or custodial environments" and warns that a leaked delegated key attached to a broad policy is dangerous ([Turnkey delegated access](https://docs.turnkey.com/features/policies/delegated-access/overview)).

This report's recommended configuration follows the researchers' conservative design: the user's own authentication is required for every interactive send and swap, the company's backend cannot initiate a transfer, any automation uses a user-granted, narrowly scoped and revocable delegation, and key export stays available. The PIN survives as an app-layer step-up in front of signing. Whether regulators accept a scoped delegation as non-custodial is not established in any source found. Privy was chosen over Turnkey for breadth and pricing shape: on list prices with ten signatures per active wallet, Turnkey's public per-signature rates cost about $500 a month at 1,000 active wallets against Privy's $299, and Turnkey requires an Enterprise contract above 2,000 wallets (researcher arithmetic on [Turnkey pricing](https://www.turnkey.com/pricing) and [Privy pricing](https://www.privy.io/pricing)). Turnkey's deeper in-enclave Solana policy inspection is the reason it is the fallback rather than an also-ran. Switching wallet vendors later is costly: a Helius guide says Privy wallets cannot be imported into its Turnkey-based product, so each user gets a new wallet and must move funds ([Helius docs](https://www.helius.dev/docs/waas/migrating-from-privy)).

### Swaps need three to five integrations behind one internal interface

Jupiter's fee rules shape the Solana design. On `/order`, an integrator fee must be 50 to 255 basis points and Jupiter keeps 20% of it; on `/build` there is no Jupiter cut but the team must land the transaction itself ([Jupiter Swap overview](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap)). A markup below 50 basis points therefore forces the `/build` path. For comparison, Phantom charges 0.85% on most swaps ([Phantom help](https://help.phantom.com/articles/understanding-cross-chain-swaps-in-phantom-51384804802195)) and Rabby 0.25% ([CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/crypto-wallets/rabby-wallet-review/)), while Relay's own platform fee tops out at 0.15% ([Relay fees](https://docs.relay.link/references/api/api_core_concepts/fees)).

Every route class should have two independent providers, and the provider layer should sit behind an internal interface with a kill switch. The reason is concrete: Odos, a DEX aggregator, announced its wind-down on 23 July 2026 and retired its production API a week later ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/odos-protocol-shut-down-4-years)); THORChain halted after an exploit of about $10.7M in May 2026 ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/05/15/thorchain-halts-trading-after-usd10-million-cross-chain-exploit-rune-token-drops-12)); Maya halted in August 2026 with no report found that swaps resumed ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/maya-protocol-1-7m-exploit-network-halt)). THORChain should never be the only route for a pair and Maya should stay off the list until a restart and audit are confirmed (researcher inference). NEAR Intents is a plausible later addition for Bitcoin and Tron, but a user report of a 575,830 TRX deposit stuck without refund is unanswered ([near/intents issue #358](https://github.com/near/intents/issues/358)), so its refund path needs testing first.

Cross-chain swaps turn a synchronous call into a multi-hour workflow. Each provider has its own status vocabulary, and LI.FI's own pages disagree on where a refund appears ([endpoint specs](https://docs.li.fi/agents/reference/endpoint-specs); [concepts](https://docs.li.fi/agents/concepts)). The researchers' design, which is inference from provider documentation because the best-practice search did not run, is one internal state machine with outcomes for pending, completed, completed in a different asset, refunded and manual review; quote and refund address persisted before the source transaction is sent; and final status taken from the chain, not from the provider.

### The launchpad should rent an audited program

Building on Meteora DBC or Raydium LaunchLab removes the need to write and audit a bonding-curve program. DBC takes 20% of the trading fee for the protocol and leaves 80% claimable by the partner ([DBC fees](https://docs.meteora.ag/core-products/dbc/fees/overview.md)); LaunchLab adds a platform fee capped at 500 basis points since 26 August 2026 on top of Raydium's protocol fee ([Raydium changelog](https://docs.raydium.io/reference/changelog/2026-08-26-launchlab-platform-fee-rate-cap.md)). pump.fun offers no partner programme that was found, so it is a competitor and a price benchmark at 1.25% on the curve ([pump.fun fees](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)), not infrastructure. An own program is a 1-3 week audit at roughly $15K-45K per auditor-team week ([Accretion](https://accretion.xyz/blog/solana-audit-cost)), repeated for each material upgrade, and the best-documented launchpad losses came from authority misuse and migration logic rather than curve math ([The Block on pump.fun](https://www.theblock.co/post/295029/pump-fun-post-mortem); [Lunaray on Four.meme](https://lunaray.medium.com/four-meme-hack-analysis-7f320dc99b9e)).

The launchpad should stay Solana-only at first and be treated as a separable, geo-gated module that ships after the wallet and swap core. EVM equivalents exist (Clanker, Zora Coins, Doppler, Flaunch), and Doppler is the only one found documenting both EVM and Solana SDKs ([doppler-sdk](https://github.com/whetstoneresearch/doppler-sdk)), but its usage was not verified. The front end's create-token response returns `bondingCurve`, `tokenVault` and `solVault` accounts (repo: `apps/foundation-dashboard/src/services/api.ts`), which suggests the lost backend ran its own program; whether that program still exists on-chain, who holds its authority and whether it was audited are unknown.

### Fiat stays with a licensed ramp; the backend only mints sessions and checks webhooks

Transak now requires partners to create each widget URL from their backend; the URL is single-use and valid for five minutes ([Transak Create Widget URL](https://docs.transak.com/api/public/create-widget-url)). MoonPay requires a server-side signature whenever the URL carries a wallet address ([MoonPay URL signing](https://dev.moonpay.com/widget/on-ramp/customization/url-signing)). Credits must come only from provider webhooks or verified on-chain transfers, which the repository's own requirements already demand (repo: `docs/BACKEND_REQUIREMENTS.md`, item 10). Keeping the ramp as the regulated counterparty, with fiat never touching a company account and crypto delivered straight to the user's address, is the condition under which commentators say the fiat leg is the provider's regulated activity ([ND Labs](https://ndlabs.dev/crypto-wallet-license)); that is a vendor and commentator position, not a regulatory ruling. The cheapest stablecoin rails (Coinbase zero-fee USDC, Stripe Onramp, Bridge) all need an application or a sales conversation and cannot be assumed on day one ([CDP Onramp FAQ](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/onramp/additional-resources/faq); [Stripe crypto overview](https://docs.stripe.com/crypto/overview)).

### Money-grade correctness is the backend's real workload

The audit calls the backend "the security boundary" and "the largest remaining risk" (repo: `docs/AUDIT.md`). The requirements file lists 29 items, 12 of them required before real funds: scoped sessions, PIN step-up and lockout, idempotency, slippage enforcement, routing by token address rather than symbol, no client-reported credits, and exact decimal arithmetic (repo: `docs/BACKEND_REQUIREMENTS.md`). The patterns that satisfy them are a double-entry ledger, stored idempotency keys, a transactional outbox, deduplicated webhook intake and reconciliation against chain state. The researchers verified ledger material but could not open the canonical idempotency and outbox references, so those design details in the notes are background knowledge to confirm against Stripe's and microservices.io's documentation.

With user-owned on-chain wallets, balances live on the chain and the ledger's scope narrows to platform fee income, rewards, staking obligations, ramp orders and swap state (this report's inference); under a custodial model it would also carry every customer liability. A 6-digit PIN cannot be protected by hashing alone because the space is one million values, so online attempt limits and a server-side secret outside the database are the real controls (researcher reasoning). The team should write new Solana code on `@solana/kit`, which shipped four major versions between October 2025 and August 2026, and pin exact versions ([npm: @solana/kit](https://registry.npmjs.org/@solana%2fkit)).

## The front-end contract must change in chain identity, amounts and who signs

The dashboard assumes 79 REST endpoints plus one OAuth redirect, and Solana at every layer (repo: `apps/foundation-dashboard/src/services/api.ts`). The preview API answers all 80 routes with typed sample data and real validation, and returns 403 on every value-moving route, so it works as an executable contract and a seed for black-box tests (repo: `apps/preview-api/src/server/app.test.ts`). The left and middle columns below are from direct reading of the repository; the right column is the researchers' design inference.

| Area | What the front end assumes today | What a multi-chain contract needs |
|---|---|---|
| Chain identity | One build-time `NEXT_PUBLIC_SOLANA_NETWORK`; explorer links, fee reserve and decimals hard-coded | A chain id on every address, asset, transaction, quote and contact, and a `GET /chains` endpoint returning native asset, validation hint, explorer templates, confirmation policy and fee reserve |
| Wallet | One wallet with one base58 `publicKey` and one numeric SOL `balance`; the UI uses `wallets[0]` | A wallet as a container of per-chain accounts; EVM chains share an address; Receive asks for chain and asset first |
| Assets | `mint` or symbol; decimals from a hard-coded map defaulting to 9; `/tokens/prices` keyed by symbol | A chain-qualified asset id; `decimals` always returned; prices and balances keyed by asset id |
| Amounts | JSON floats almost everywhere | Decimal or base-unit strings in both directions; 18-decimal EVM tokens exceed what a float holds exactly |
| Send and validation | `{toAddress, amount, tokenMint?}`; `validate-address` takes no chain; contacts capped at 44 characters with a Solana check | `chainId`, `assetId`, string amount, optional memo or tag, fee preference; validation per chain; contacts gain a chain |
| Transactions | `signature`, `SEND` or `RECEIVE`, three statuses, no pagination | `txHash`, confirmations, fee asset, richer statuses including dropped or replaced, pagination |
| Swap | Jupiter-shaped quote with `jupiterFee`; no quote id; one `blockchainTxHash`; PIN in the body | Source and destination chain, a server-issued `quoteId` with expiry, provider-neutral fee breakdown, an asynchronous `GET /swaps/:id` with leg states and refund outcome |
| Launchpad and staking | Every amount named `*Sol`; Solana accounts in responses; staking is one token with 9 decimals | Declare both Solana-only with an explicit chain and quote asset, or rename the fields |
| Market data | `/market-data/solana`; candles built client-side from trades | `/market-data/:assetId` and server-side candles |
| Fiat ramp | MoonPay fixed to `sol`; Transak offers SOL and USDC | Chain and asset on ramp URL generation, with the matching deposit address |

Three further changes are needed whichever chains are supported. First, session handling: tokens issued after OTP or Google sign-in must be "PIN pending", the OAuth callback should return a one-time code instead of a token in the URL, and moving to httpOnly cookies needs a change in `lib/session.ts`, which the code comments say was isolated for that swap (repo: `docs/BACKEND_REQUIREMENTS.md`, items 1, 13, 14). Second, onboarding: `continue-with-phone` currently returns `isNewUser`, `walletId` and `walletAddress`, which is the account-enumeration leak the requirements forbid, so the onboarding screens must change with it. Third, idempotency keys are sent on only five of the value-moving calls and are missing on stake withdrawal, token creation and reward claims (repo: `apps/foundation-dashboard/src/services/api.ts`).

The largest change follows from the custody recommendation and is this report's inference, since the vendors' SDK flows were not read: routes that today mean "server signs automatically" become prepare, sign and submit. The backend returns an unsigned transaction bound to a quote, the client signs through the wallet vendor's SDK under the user's authentication, and the backend or the aggregator broadcasts and tracks. If the owner chooses to stay custodial, those routes keep their shape and the licence programme begins instead.

The admin console also needs rework. Its `Transaction` model is an internal wallet-to-wallet transfer with a `confirmationCode`, unlike the dashboard's on-chain records, and it has no screens for swaps, staking, KYC, audit logs or wallet freezing (repo: `apps/foundation-admin/src/types/index.ts`). The notes list the front-end files that would change but do not size that work, so every duration below is backend only.

## Three to four engineers need 4-6 months for Solana and about 7-12 for four chain families

Phases 1 to 4 sum to 18-26 weeks, and phases 5 to 7 add 13-26 weeks if run one after another, which gives 31-52 weeks in total. Two engineers would roughly double the calendar time, and five would let the chain work run in parallel (researcher estimate). The design choice that keeps this from becoming two rewrites is to define the chain-neutral contract in Phase 1 even though only Solana is live until Phase 5.

External gates usually set the critical path more than engineering does: wallet-vendor onboarding, ramp and KYC production approval, legal review, the penetration test, and any audit of launchpad or staking programs (researcher inference). Using a wallet vendor and swap aggregators instead of building signing and routing in-house is the largest schedule lever available. The team also needs front-end capacity, external counsel and a penetration-test firm on top of the 3-4 backend engineers; none of those is sized in the notes.

## Four risk families, with the launchpad the most exposed feature

### Security: attackers go through the system that asks for signatures

About $3.4B was stolen from crypto platforms in 2025, and the largest theft, Bybit at about $1.5B, came through a compromised developer machine that altered a signing interface rather than through a stolen key ([The Block on Chainalysis](https://www.theblock.co/post/382477/crypto-hack-2025-chainalysis); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/380992/biggest-crypto-hacks-2025)). DMM Bitcoin lost about $305M after an employee of its wallet-software vendor was compromised through a fake recruiter approach ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20241225-dmm-bitcoin-north-korea)), and the exchange then shut down ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/amp/post/328890/japanese-exchange-dmm-bitcoin-to-shut-down-transfer-assets-to-sbi-group-unit-after-300-million-hack)). The notes also record two large 2026 exchange incidents from single or vendor sources that were not confirmed against primary statements; they are not relied on here. The lesson for this build is that a vendor enclave is necessary but not sufficient: a stolen server credential attached to a broad policy still drains wallets, so policies must be narrow, the credential must live in a secrets manager with per-service access, and the deploy pipeline and staff laptops are part of the perimeter.

The product's own audit supplies the application-level threat list: sessions issued before the PIN, PIN guessing without lockout, a login-CSRF path through the OAuth callback, client-reported balance credits, double submission, swaps not bound to their quote, and symbol-based token substitution (repo: `docs/AUDIT.md`). Dependency compromise is a first-order risk for JavaScript wallet code: in September 2025 a phished maintainer account led to 18 popular npm packages being republished with an injected payload for about two hours ([AWS Security Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/defending-against-supply-chain-attacks-like-chalk-debug-and-the-shai-hulud-worm)). The specific defences the researchers list (frozen lockfiles, exact pins, blocked lifecycle scripts, delayed adoption of new releases) are background knowledge, because that search did not run.

### Vendor: providers disappear, get bought, and change prices

Three data providers named in the original shortlist are gone: SimpleHash's public API ended in March 2025 after Phantom bought it ([Phantom blog](https://phantom.com/learn/blog/phantom-acquires-simplehash)), Syndica retired its Solana RPC in February 2026 ([Syndica status](https://status.syndica.io/incidents/bd50qjpcrqmd)), and Dune Sim shut down on 1 August 2026 ([Dune](https://dune.com/blog/sunsetting-sim)). Wallet infrastructure consolidated in the same period: Stripe acquired Privy ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/06/11/stripe-acquires-crypto-wallet-infrastructure-provider-privy/)), Fireblocks acquired Dynamic ([Fireblocks](https://www.fireblocks.com/company-news-updates/fireblocks-acquires-dynamic)), and Web3Auth, now under Consensys, paused its embedded wallet service on 6 November 2026 after an attacker reached wallets created through social login ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/web3auth-flaw-exploited-to-steal-keplr.html)). No incident reports were found for Privy or Turnkey in 2025-2026, which is an absence of search results, not proof. The mitigations are structural: one internal interface per capability, a tested fallback per route class, key export enabled, and no vendor chosen on price alone while its price sheet is in dispute.

### Regulatory: favourable but non-binding in the US, hard-edged in the EU and UK

This subsection is background, not legal advice. In the US, an SEC staff statement of 13 April 2026 says staff will not object to a non-custodial interface going unregistered if its fee is fixed, disclosed and neutral across product, route and venue, and if it handles no customer funds; the statement has "no legal force or effect", excludes custodial wallet providers, and lapses after five years ([SEC staff statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/staff-statement-regarding-broker-dealer-registration-certain-user-interfaces-utilized-prepare-staff-statement-regarding-broker-dealer-registration-certain-user-interfaces-utilized); [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260417-sec-staff-issues-broker-dealer-registration-guidance-for-certain-user-interfaces)). Legislation that would codify this stalled: a Senate cloture vote on the CLARITY Act failed 49-50 on 15 September 2026 ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/Insights/2026/10/The-CLARITY-Act-Stalls-in-the-Senate-Whats-Next-for-Digital-Asset-Regulation)). The retrial of a Tornado Cash developer convicted on a money-transmitting count is set for April 2027 ([The Block](https://theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-08-26-tornado-cash-roman-storm-retrial-april-2027-412761)), a reminder that prosecutors can still argue a fee-earning team around non-custodial software is a transmitter.

In the EU, the only carve-out for decentralised services sits in a recital, and ESMA's response of 30 September 2026 asks for it to be narrowly drawn, citing "decentralisation washing" ([HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/madrid/2026-posts/note-esma-response-ce-mica-october-2026)). Whether a company-run, fee-taking, non-custodial interface is inside MiCA is unresolved in every source found. In the UK, the financial promotions regime already reaches apps accessible to UK consumers wherever the firm is based, and breach is a criminal offence ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/good-poor-practice/firms-preparations-cryptoasset-financial-promotions-regime)); the full authorisation regime starts on 25 October 2027 with an application window that closes on 28 February 2027 ([FCA gateway](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/new-regime-cryptoasset-regulation/how-gateway-will-operate)). Sanctions law applies regardless of custody: commentary on OFAC's settlements with the wallet maker Exodus and with ShapeShift describes geo-blocking, VPN detection and address screening as the expected baseline ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2026/02/ofac-sanctions-enforcement-in-fintech-and-crypto)).

Two existing features carry their own exposure. Staking in the front end is a single-token product with fixed lock periods and stated yields (repo: `apps/preview-api/src/server/data/account.ts`); the researchers infer that pooled staking with promised yields moves towards custody in the EU and an investment contract in the US. And the stablecoins offered to EU users are a compliance variable since ESMA's October 2026 opinion on non-MiCA-compliant tokens ([ESMA opinion](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-10/ESMA75-113276571-1742%5FOpinion%5Fon%5Fthe%5Fprovision%5Fof%5Fcrypto%5Fasset%5Fservices%5Fin%5Frelation%5Fto%5Fnon-MiCA-compliant%5Fasset-referenced%5Ftokens%5Fand%5Fe-money%5Ftokens.pdf)).

### Launchpad: litigation follows the operator, not the code

On 31 August 2026 a New York federal court dismissed securities claims against pump.fun's operator for two tokens but let civil RICO claims against the operator and its three founders proceed to discovery ([SDNY opinion](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.635992/gov.uscourts.nysd.635992.184.0.pdf)). The surviving theory targets the operator's conduct, so renting Meteora's or Raydium's program does not reduce that exposure (researcher inference). The outcome data feeds the reputational risk: about 1 in 58 pump.fun launches graduated and 79% of coins were dead after day one in the year to October 2026 ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/solana-launchpad-year)). Apple's guideline 3.1.5 restricts apps that facilitate token offerings to established financial institutions ([FinanceFeeds](https://financefeeds.com/apples-crypto-policy-explained/)), which makes a web app the safer distribution route for this feature.

Technical risks remain even on a rented program. Meteora upgrades DBC itself and has already deprecated features, so the team must track its developer channel and pin the SDK ([DBC launch configurations](https://docs.meteora.ag/core-products/dbc/launch-configurations)). LaunchLab usage is concentrated, with a single partner accounting for 95% of launches in September 2026 ([Pine Analytics](https://pineanalytics.substack.com/p/raydium-quarterly-report-q3-2026)). The audit flagged the old hard-coded 5% slippage as a sandwiching risk on thin curves (repo: `docs/AUDIT.md`). Safeguards such as creator identity checks, content moderation and bundle detection were not researched.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question the owner is asking. "Can we become a multi-chain exchange?" turns out to be mostly a question about who is allowed to sign, and only secondarily about chains: adding EVM, Bitcoin and Tron is weeks of adapter work each once the contract is chain-neutral, whereas staying custodial adds a licence programme measured in years and a class of risk that has ended regulated exchanges. The lost backend is, in that sense, an opportunity. The front end has to change anyway to fix its session and money-handling flaws, so the team can move the signing step to the user at the same time and avoid building, and later dismantling, a custodial core.

The plan is also more fragile than its tables suggest. It rests on list prices nobody on the project has read first-hand, on a category of vendors in which several shut down or changed hands within eighteen months, and on regulatory positions that are staff-level in the US and unsettled in the EU and UK. The sensible first month therefore produces no product code: it produces a custody and jurisdiction decision with counsel, a verified price sheet from the live vendor pages, an inventory of what survives on-chain from the old system, and a contract-test suite lifted from the preview API. Every estimate in this report should be re-issued after that month.
